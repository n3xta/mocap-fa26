Recent reports show that human labor is increasingly becoming part of the training infrastructure for AI and robotics. In Wuhan, workers wearing VR equipment repeatedly perform actions such as bending, grabbing components, packing boxes, and moving objects; their movements are captured and converted into data for humanoid robots to imitate ([The Paper, 2026](https://m.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_33247554)). Similar “data factories” are appearing across China, where human trainers repeat everyday tasks such as folding clothes, wiping tables, stacking objects, and opening appliances to generate robot-training data ([Rest of World, Jan. 2026](https://restofworld.org/2026/china-robots-training-centers-workers/); [Rest of World, Jun. 2026](https://restofworld.org/2026/china-ai-robotics-training-data/)). At Agibot’s Shanghai facility, this embodied data is not only used internally but can also be sold as a product to other companies ([SCMP, 2026](https://www.scmp.com/tech/big-tech/article/3351817/hype-or-real-cash-flow-chinas-robot-boom-faces-reality-check-commercialisation-lags)). Data labor also extends beyond physical movement: Chinese vocational-school students have been employed to perform repetitive annotation work for AI systems, sometimes as mandatory internships ([Rest of World, 2023](https://restofworld.org/2023/china-ai-student-labor/)), while lawyers, architects, engineers, and other professionals are now being hired to teach AI systems tasks based on their own professional knowledge ([Rest of World, Sep. 2026](https://restofworld.org/2026/china-expert-ai-trainers/)). Xiaomi’s MiMo team has also made part of this process unusually visible by publicly livestreaming the reinforcement-learning progress of MiMo-V2.6, exposing the normally hidden computational process behind model training ([The Paper, Sep. 17, 2026](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34088356?commTag=true)).
